Getting students on board with retrieval

Explain, demonstrate and build habits

Use an explanation and demonstration of retrieval when students see quizzes as judgement or prefer familiar rereading. Help them understand why attempting recall is part of learning.

How to do it

  1. Identify the concern. Find out whether students fear scores, do not understand the purpose or lack a useful retrieval routine.
  2. Explain simply. Describe how trying to recall differs from rereading and why feedback follows the attempt.
  3. Demonstrate with accessible content. Let students experience independent recall and checking without making the task a test of self-worth.
  4. Establish the routine. Use predictable instructions, achievable demands and respectful responses to errors.
  5. Reinforce the learning purpose. Show how previous gaps return in future practice and how students can use retrieval independently.

What it sounds like

“You may recognise this when reading it, but can you produce it without the page? Try first; the gap tells us what to practise, and the feedback helps repair it.”

Do

  • Explain the purpose and give corrective feedback.
  • Use mistakes to guide future practice.

Avoid

  • A public ranking that undermines the low-stakes message.
  • Claiming effortful failure is always beneficial.

Top tips

  • Explain the learning purpose. A low-stakes attempt strengthens access to knowledge and shows what to revisit.
  • Demonstrate a fair comparison. Use a simple experience to contrast recognition with generating an answer, without promising an instant universal result.
  • Normalise effort. Difficulty recalling is information about the learning, not a reason to avoid the attempt.
  • Make success attainable. Use well-taught content and a useful prompt where total failure would prevent productive practice.
  • Keep the routine consistent. Repeated experiences of attempt, feedback and improvement build more trust than a single motivational speech.

In the classroom

A teacher explains why recognising a paragraph is different from producing its key idea without looking. Pupils try an accessible recall task, receive feedback and meet the same knowledge again later. The routine demonstrates a learning purpose and treats errors respectfully, helping build trust in retrieval beyond a single explanation.

Use it for Individual Development

Example goal

I will explain and demonstrate why retrieval works, so that students attempt recall before checking notes and use retrieval when revising independently.

Peer observer look-fors

  • The teacher explains briefly why recall beats rereading.
  • Pupils experience it (for example, a short demonstration or comparison).
  • Pupils describe or use retrieval when revising.

Quick check of impact

Ask pupils how they revise. Note how many describe self-testing rather than rereading.

Adapt the goal to your class and subject. See writing a precise goal and peer observation.
