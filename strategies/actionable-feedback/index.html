Feedback to improve · FI01

Actionable feedback

Giving students a useful next step

Use individual feedback when a precise next action can improve work while the thinking is still live. Make the location, learning issue and required change clear.

This strategy applies an approach with a strong, consistent research base, such as high-security EEF evidence or well-replicated findings from cognitive science. Research on this exact routine may be more limited, so check how your pupils respond.

How to do it

  1. Plan and break the plane early. Name the success criteria, must-check pupils and a predictable route before work begins.
  2. Cover the full field. Move systematically, stay aware of the room and avoid reactive zig-zagging or repeatedly visiting the same pupils.
  3. Diagnose before feeding back. Scan, read and compare with the criteria; note exemplars, partial attempts, misconceptions and pupils to revisit.
  4. Use prompt, clue, then direct. Start with the least support likely to activate the pupil's knowledge; escalate only when the response shows it is needed.
  5. Bound the action. Identify the exact location and a finite improvement that the pupil can complete now.
  6. Return and respond. Check the change, praise against criteria and pause for whole-class correction when the same misconception is widespread.

What it sounds like

“Your claim is clear. In this sentence, add evidence that supports it. Tell me what you will change, then make the edit and I will return.”

Do

  • Name the exact feature and next action.
  • Check listening and require a response.

Avoid

  • Vague praise followed by “more detail”.
  • Giving the answer when a usable prompt would suffice.

Top tips

  • Begin with the successful feature. Precise recognition tells the pupil what to retain while improving another part.
  • Use prompt, clue, direct. Start with a question if the pupil has the knowledge; provide more guidance when the response shows it is needed.
  • Bound the action. One sentence or process step is easier to act on than “improve your explanation”.
  • Ask for immediate work. A feedback conversation should end with the student thinking, writing, rehearsing or trying again.
  • Return later. Check the change and an additional attempt rather than assuming the comment was understood.

In the classroom

In PE, a pupil receives feedback on one feature of a taught movement. The teacher identifies what is already successful, gives a precise next action and asks for another attempt. A later return checks the change. The feedback is short enough to use immediately and does not become a list of every possible improvement.

Use it for Individual Development

Example goal

I will give one precise next step that students act on straight away, so that students improve their work while the thinking is still fresh.

Peer observer look-fors

  • Feedback identifies a specific next step.
  • Pupils act on it during the lesson.
  • The improved work shows the change.

Quick check of impact

Compare the original and improved work for the targeted change.

Adapt the goal to your class and subject. See writing a precise goal and peer observation.
