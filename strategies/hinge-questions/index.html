Hinge questions

Deciding whether to move on

Use a hinge question at a decision point where students’ answers should change what happens next. Plan the question and the teaching response together.

How to do it

  1. Plan the hinge in advance. Choose a pivotal concept and build plausible distractors from common misconceptions rather than random errors.
  2. Ask it after a small teaching chunk. Place the question just before the lesson would otherwise move into the next explanation or practice demand.
  3. Protect independent commitment. Give silent thinking time, keep the answer hidden and collect a whole-class response before partner discussion where initial diagnosis matters.
  4. Scan choices and reasoning. Look for patterns, then invite pupils to explain why an option was attractive before revealing the correct answer.
  5. Make the instructional decision. Move on when understanding is sufficiently secure; otherwise re-explain the identified misconception, use a different example or extend guided practice.
  6. Recheck and refine. Test the same concept in a related form and later improve distractors that did not reveal the intended thinking.

What it sounds like

“Choose privately and prepare your reason. We will use the responses to decide whether we are ready for the next example or need to revisit this distinction.”

Do

  • Place the question before a consequential increase in demand.
  • Inspect reasoning and the pattern of choices.

Avoid

  • A quiz item with no effect on the next teaching move.
  • Automatically moving on when a fixed percentage is correct.

Top tips

  • Identify the hinge. Choose a concept that students need before the next task or explanation will make sense.
  • Match each distractor to an error. An option should tell you what needs clarifying, not just whether the answer is wrong.
  • Collect independent answers first. Discussion can improve learning afterwards, but initial private commitment gives cleaner diagnostic evidence.
  • Decide your likely responses. Plan what you will do for a common misconception, mixed understanding and broadly secure answers.
  • Ask a fresh follow-up. The response after repair should require application, not memorisation of the original option.

In the classroom

Before a new stage in a calculation, a hinge question checks the relationship needed for that stage. Each option represents a different likely misconception. Independent choices reveal a shared error, so the teacher pauses, contrasts the methods and asks a fresh question. The planned next task changes because of the evidence.

Use it for Individual Development

Example goal

I will plan a hinge question at a key point in the lesson, so that I decide whether to move on or reteach based on evidence from every student.

Peer observer look-fors

  • The question is quick to answer and diagnostic.
  • Every pupil responds simultaneously.
  • The teacher's next step depends on the results.

Quick check of impact

Record hinge results and later task success to see whether the decision was right.

Adapt the goal to your class and subject. See writing a precise goal and peer observation.
