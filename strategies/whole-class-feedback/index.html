Whole-class feedback

Responding to patterns in work

Use whole-class feedback when students’ work reveals shared strengths and recurring difficulties. Review the pattern once, then give everyone a useful improvement task.

This strategy is consistent with research principles, but the evidence for this kind of practice is less direct, more mixed or more dependent on context.

How to do it

  1. Review student work. Read the relevant body of work to identify common strengths, misconceptions and repeated errors; avoid annotating every piece in detail.
  2. Record key feedback. Use a crib sheet for success criteria, what went well, model answers, misconceptions, key vocabulary, SPaG patterns and next actions.
  3. Plan the response. Choose a model, comparison, live edit, brief reteach, discussion or practice task that will turn the pattern into learning.
  4. Deliver feedback visibly. Use the board, visualiser, screen or handout to show strong examples and typical errors without exposing or embarrassing pupils.
  5. Get pupils thinking and improving. Allocate time for a focused response: redraft, rehearse, revisit, re-learn or research, according to the learning need.
  6. Reflect and respond. Check the improvements, decide what requires future teaching and revisit fragile learning through practice, retrieval or a hinge question.

What it sounds like

“This feature was secure across many responses. This next error appeared often, so we will model it, try a related question and then improve our own work.”

Do

  • Choose the response from reviewed student work.
  • Allocate time for students to act.

Avoid

  • A presentation of comments with no improvement task.
  • Reteaching every error for the same amount of time.

Top tips

  • Select a small number of high-value issues. Two or three important misconceptions can be more useful than reviewing every mark lost.
  • Prepare before returning scores. Deliver the model and recheck while attention is on learning rather than individual results.
  • Use barometer work carefully. Selected work can reveal patterns, but do not assume a small sample captures every need.
  • Model, recheck, stretch. Use a fresh equivalent question after the explanation, then a useful variation when secure.
  • Make errors retrievable. Review Cards turn selected individual gaps into future practice, beyond today’s correction.

In the classroom

An English assessment reveals a recurring weak explanation after evidence. The teacher selects a model and near-miss, improves the connection live and asks pupils to complete a fresh equivalent paragraph. Work is then returned for an individual improvement task. Feedback becomes instruction and practice rather than repeated comments on many scripts.

Use it for Individual Development

Example goal

I will use whole-class feedback on common strengths and errors followed by an improvement task, so that all students act on feedback efficiently.

Peer observer look-fors

  • The teacher shares common patterns from the work.
  • Pupils complete a task to improve their work.
  • Improvements are visible in pupils' work.

Quick check of impact

Look for the targeted improvement in pupils' redrafted or next work.

Adapt the goal to your class and subject. See writing a precise goal and peer observation.
