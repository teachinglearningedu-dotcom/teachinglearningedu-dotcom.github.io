Checks for listening

Instructions, explanations and classmates

Use brief checks to establish whether students heard the instruction, key explanation point or contribution from a classmate. This helps distinguish missed information from difficulty understanding it.

How to do it

  1. Plan the message. Identify the exact instruction, definition or relationship students need to retain and make it concise.
  2. Establish the routine. Explain why listening will be checked, secure attention and deliver the message without competing activity.
  3. Ask for recall. Use question–pause–name, choral response or a brief written response to retrieve what was actually said.
  4. Repair the missed point. Re-deliver or reframe it clearly, then check again; ask students to paraphrase a peer’s contribution where appropriate.
  5. Move to understanding. Give a fresh question requiring application or explanation. Students can repeat accurately while remaining unsure how to use the idea.

What it sounds like

“A catalyst speeds up a reaction without being used up. A catalyst speeds up…? Something that speeds up a reaction without being used up is…?”

Do

  • Check instructions, explanations and classmates.
  • Ask a separate application question afterwards.

Avoid

  • Using “Are you listening?” as the check.
  • Assuming successful repetition establishes understanding.

Top tips

  • Teach the distinction. Explain that this question checks what was just said; a later task will check whether students can apply it.
  • Use Explain–Frame–Reframe. State a key relationship, ask students to complete it, then reverse the prompt where the reversal remains accurate.
  • Check peers’ contributions. Ask another student to restate the idea before inviting agreement, challenge or elaboration.
  • Make the message accessible first. Secure quiet, speak audibly and avoid distributing resources or introducing competing text while delivering it.
  • Keep corrections supportive. Restate the short message and give another opportunity; hearing, language or processing difficulties must not be treated as deliberate inattention.

In the classroom

An English teacher explains that the claim must be supported with a quotation. A pupil states where the evidence should appear, then another restates the key point from a classmate’s answer. Those checks establish receipt. A new paragraph task then tests whether students can select suitable evidence and explain the connection independently.

Use it for Individual Development

Example goal

I will check that students have heard key instructions and classmates' contributions by asking a quick follow-up, so that students listen actively and act on what was said.

Peer observer look-fors

  • After an instruction, the teacher asks one or two pupils to repeat it before the task starts.
  • Pupils are asked to build on or summarise a classmate's answer.
  • Fewer pupils ask what to do once the task begins.

Quick check of impact

Count the questions about what to do in the first two minutes of tasks, before and after.

Adapt the goal to your class and subject. See writing a precise goal and peer observation.
