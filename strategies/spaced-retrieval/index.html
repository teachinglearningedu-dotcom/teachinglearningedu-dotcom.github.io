Spaced retrieval

Revisiting knowledge over time

Use spaced retrieval when students can answer soon after teaching but struggle weeks later. Plan repeated returns to important knowledge rather than treating a topic as finished.

How to do it

  1. Identify the durable knowledge. Select the curriculum content students will need to use again, especially prerequisites for later topics.
  2. Map return points. Include an initial practice opportunity and later retrieval across lessons, quizzes and homework.
  3. Vary the cues carefully. Ask for the same knowledge through a definition, example, diagram or application without suddenly testing untaught content.
  4. Respond to forgetting. Explain the important error, provide an accurate model and give another independent retrieval opportunity.
  5. Adjust the next return. Revisit fragile items sooner and maintain occasional retrieval of secure knowledge; do not use a rigid interval regardless of success.

What it sounds like

“This question returns to a topic from three weeks ago. Try it from memory before using the worked solution.”

Do

  • Plan returns across lessons and units.
  • Include both recent and older important knowledge.

Avoid

  • Leaving all revision until the end of a unit.
  • Assuming one delayed quiz is sufficient for every student.

Top tips

  • Space the learning, not just the timetable. A later prompt should require students to recall the idea again; displaying the answer beforehand removes that opportunity.
  • Expect effort. Some forgetting makes retrieval demanding, but repeated near-total failure suggests the delay or prompt needs adjusting.
  • Keep a curriculum record. Track what has been retrieved and which ideas need another return so selection is deliberate.
  • Combine spacing with feedback. Correct errors before the next attempt; do not repeatedly rehearse the same wrong answer.
  • Distinguish spacing from interleaving. Spacing concerns time between encounters; interleaving concerns mixing problem types that require selection.

In the classroom

An English team maps several returns to important terminology across a unit. Pupils first retrieve definitions, later identify examples and later explain the term in a new text. Incorrect responses receive feedback before another attempt. The returns are spaced across learning rather than concentrated into a single revision lesson before the assessment.

Use it for Individual Development

Example goal

I will plan returns to key knowledge at increasing intervals, so that students can recall it weeks later, not just soon after teaching.

Peer observer look-fors

  • Retrieval questions include content from several weeks or terms ago.
  • The teacher can say why these items were chosen now.
  • Pupils show recall of older content, and gaps are noted for future returns.

Quick check of impact

Re-test a few items four to six weeks after first teaching. Compare with earlier cohorts or topics.

Adapt the goal to your class and subject. See writing a precise goal and peer observation.
