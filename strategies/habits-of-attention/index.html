Habits of attention

Teaching purposeful listening routines

Use habits of attention to teach where students should direct attention during explanation, modelling and discussion. Each habit needs a learning purpose and a recognisable cue.

How to do it

  1. Explain the learning purpose. Connect the habit to hearing a step, following a model, respecting a speaker or reducing distraction—not obedience for its own sake.
  2. Select the highest-value behaviour. Teach only what matters in the current phase, such as tracking a model during explanation or orienting to a peer during discussion.
  3. Model and rehearse briefly. Show what the habit looks and sounds like, practise once and return quickly to the learning.
  4. Reinforce with short cues. Prompt early and quietly, narrate success occasionally and avoid turning the routine into constant commentary.
  5. Apply selectively and adapt the form. Turn the habit off when pupils should read or write; make reasonable adjustments while preserving the attentional purpose.
  6. Fade towards independence. Reduce verbal prompts to a gesture or question so pupils increasingly manage their own attention.

What it sounds like

“For this demonstration, put your pen down and attend to the diagram. Afterwards I will ask you to explain what changed between the two stages.”

Do

  • Teach the action and its purpose.
  • Make the change of attentional focus explicit.

Avoid

  • Enforcing a posture checklist for its own sake.
  • Expecting attentional routines to compensate for unclear explanation.

Top tips

  • Explain the purpose. A listening or viewing action is useful because it supports access to the idea, not because it looks compliant.
  • Name the relevant focus. Attention may belong on the model, speaker, diagram or text at different moments.
  • Practise the transition. Students need to switch attention fluently between writing, talk and shared explanation.
  • Check the message. Recall or application tells you more than posture, silence or nodding.
  • Avoid a rigid posture test. Use accessible ways of attending and do not treat direct eye contact as the only valid evidence.

In the classroom

In Drama, pupils shift from performance rehearsal to watching a demonstration. The teacher specifies the feature to attend to, then asks pupils to recall the key action and apply it in rehearsal. The attention habit supports access to the demonstration; visible posture alone is not treated as evidence that the performance technique was learned.

Use it for Individual Development

Example goal

I will teach and reinforce one habit of attention with a clear learning purpose, so that students track the speaker and engage with explanations and discussion.

Peer observer look-fors

  • The habit is explained in terms of learning, not compliance.
  • The teacher reinforces it with brief, positive narration.
  • Pupils' attention follows the speaker or the model during key moments.

Quick check of impact

Sample whether pupils can summarise what a speaker or the teacher just said.

Adapt the goal to your class and subject. See writing a precise goal and peer observation.
