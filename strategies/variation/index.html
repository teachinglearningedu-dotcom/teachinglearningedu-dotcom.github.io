Variation

Changing examples to deepen understanding

Use variation to help students notice the structure of an idea rather than copy surface features. Decide what changes, what stays the same and why that comparison matters.

How to do it

  1. Choose the relationship. Identify the feature, rule or structure students should notice across examples.
  2. Establish a baseline. Solve or explain one accessible example so the comparison has a clear starting point.
  3. Change a consequential feature. Keep other demands stable and ask students to predict the effect.
  4. Compare the outcomes. Discuss what changed and what remained valid, using the underlying principle rather than appearance.
  5. Test the generalisation. Present a less familiar case and ask students to justify whether the same relationship still applies.

What it sounds like

“Only the direction of the arrow has changed. Predict what that changes in the explanation, and identify what remains true.”

Do

  • Select changes that reveal the target relationship.
  • Ask students to describe the invariant.

Avoid

  • Changing several unfamiliar features at once.
  • Assuming a varied worksheet automatically produces conceptual understanding.

Top tips

  • Change deliberately. A sequence is not purposeful variation simply because every question looks different.
  • Include an unchanged relationship. Students need something stable against which to notice the new feature.
  • Ask for a prediction. Before calculation or completion, ask what they expect to change and why.
  • Use near-misses. A small change that makes a case invalid can clarify a concept boundary.
  • Compare after solving. Ask students to explain the effect of the change rather than moving straight to another item.

In the classroom

A Science teacher changes one condition in a familiar investigation and asks pupils to predict the consequence before calculating or explaining. Pupils compare the new outcome with the baseline and identify the relationship that remains true. A further example tests the generalisation, so variation directs attention to structure rather than mere novelty.

Use it for Individual Development

Example goal

I will vary one feature at a time across practice examples, so that students notice the underlying structure rather than copying surface features.

Peer observer look-fors

  • Examples change one element systematically.
  • The teacher draws attention to what changed and what stayed the same.
  • Pupils explain how the change affects the answer.

Quick check of impact

Give a novel example of the same structure. Check whether pupils transfer correctly.

Adapt the goal to your class and subject. See writing a precise goal and peer observation.
