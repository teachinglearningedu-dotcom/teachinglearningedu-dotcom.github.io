Storytelling and analogies

Making an explanation coherent and memorable

Use a story or analogy when it clarifies a sequence, relationship or abstract idea. Keep the narrative tied to the disciplinary meaning students must learn.

Evidence: Established practice

This strategy is mainly based on expert practitioner experience and agreed BSCS routines. Research supports the underlying principles indirectly.

Read the evidence summary:

  • Cognitive science approaches: retrieval, spacing, cognitive load, worked examples and dual coding

How to do it

  1. Identify the relationship. Decide what the story or comparison will make easier to understand.
  2. Select a familiar structure. Use a short sequence or analogy students can follow without extensive new background.
  3. Explain the mapping. Link the important narrative features to the subject concept and show the cause or relationship.
  4. State the boundary. Identify a way the analogy differs from the target idea before that difference becomes a misconception.
  5. Translate back. Ask students to explain the concept in disciplinary terms and apply it to an example outside the original story.

What it sounds like

“Think of this as a pressure cooker building tension. In the text, what represents the pressure? Where does the analogy help, and where does it stop being accurate?”

Do

  • Choose a narrative that carries the intended explanation.
  • Connect it back to precise vocabulary.

Avoid

  • A memorable story with an unclear learning point.
  • Treating an analogy as literally identical to the concept.

Top tips

  • Keep cause and consequence visible. A story should help explain why an event or change occurs, not merely entertain.
  • Map the analogy explicitly. State what each part represents in the target idea.
  • Name the limits. Tell students where the familiar comparison stops being accurate.
  • Remove unnecessary detail. Interesting characters or episodes can become the remembered feature instead of the concept.
  • Return to subject language. Students need to explain the idea without relying on the story alone.

In the classroom

An English teacher uses a short analogy to explain mounting tension in a text. Each part of the comparison is mapped to evidence, and a limitation is named. Pupils then explain the tension using subject vocabulary and quotations. The analogy helps access the idea without becoming a substitute for precise textual analysis.

Use it for Individual Development

Example goal

I will use a short story or analogy that maps clearly onto the key idea, so that students remember the explanation and explain the concept accurately.

Peer observer look-fors

  • The story or analogy is tied explicitly to the subject meaning.
  • The teacher points out where the analogy breaks down.
  • Pupils use the analogy correctly when explaining.

Quick check of impact

Check whether pupils' explanations use the analogy accurately and avoid its limits.

Adapt the goal to your class and subject. See writing a precise goal and peer observation.
