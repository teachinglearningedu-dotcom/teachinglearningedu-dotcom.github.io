Clear instructions and front-loading

Specify participation before the task

Use front-loading when students begin responding or moving before they have heard the participation instructions. Say how to respond before giving the content that will capture attention.

How to do it

  1. Plan the action. Define the response location, participation mode, stopping point and what students should do if finished or stuck.
  2. Secure attention. Use the shared signal and wait until students can access the instruction.
  3. Front-load participation. State how students will respond before presenting the interesting question, object or task.
  4. Check receipt. Ask a student to state the first action or a key restriction, clarifying briefly where needed.
  5. Release clearly. Use the agreed cue, display any useful reminder and observe the first actions before moving into individual support.

What it sounds like

“In silence, write one answer on your board and keep it hidden. What are the two response rules? Now the question: which source is more reliable?”

Do

  • Specify where, how and when students respond.
  • Check listening before students act.

Avoid

  • Asking the question and adding instructions afterwards.
  • Repeating every direction automatically without finding out what was missed.

Top tips

  • Put participation first. “On your board, silently…” comes before the question, not after an answer has been called out.
  • Keep the instructions numbered. Students can retrieve the first action and the checking point more easily than a long paragraph of directions.
  • Use an Instruction Sandwich. State the instruction, check that students heard it, then restate the concise version before release where useful.
  • Display complex directions afterwards. A stable reminder can support action without competing with the original spoken message.
  • Use a clear release cue. “When I say go” separates listening from movement and prevents the first instruction being lost.

In the classroom

Before displaying a diagnostic question, the teacher states that pupils will choose silently on boards and keep answers hidden. A listening check retrieves those rules. The question is then shown and the class commits privately. The interesting content has not arrived before the participation instructions, reducing unwanted calling out and contaminated responses.

Use it for Individual Development

Example goal

I will say how students should respond before I give the question or task, so that students start correctly and fewer call out or start too early.

Peer observer look-fors

  • The teacher gives the participation instruction first (for example, "On your boards, silently...").
  • Instructions are short, in order and checked before release.
  • Pupils respond in the specified way straight away.

Quick check of impact

Count call-outs and false starts during task launches.

Adapt the goal to your class and subject. See writing a precise goal and peer observation.
