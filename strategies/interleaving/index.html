Interleaving

Practising how to choose an approach

Use interleaving when students can perform several approaches separately but need practice deciding which one fits. Mix types that require a meaningful choice after initial teaching.

Research-informed

Evidence: Research-informed

This strategy is consistent with research principles, but the evidence for this kind of practice is less direct, more mixed or more dependent on context.

Read the evidence summary:

  • Cognitive science approaches: retrieval, spacing, cognitive load, worked examples and dual coding

How we rate evidence

How to do it

  1. Check initial competence. Confirm that students can use each approach in a familiar form with reasonable accuracy.
  2. Select contrasting types. Choose tasks for which students must discriminate between related methods or concepts.
  3. Mix the sequence. Avoid blocks that reveal which method comes next, while keeping demands manageable.
  4. Require the choice. Ask students to identify the approach and explain the clue before or alongside completing the response.
  5. Give comparative feedback. Address why an alternative did not fit, then revisit the choice in later mixed practice.

What it sounds like

“These questions need different methods. Before calculating, name the method you would choose and the feature in the question that tells you.”

Do

  • Practise selection as well as execution.
  • Keep the approaches individually accessible.

Avoid

  • Mixing untaught content into independent practice.
  • Giving a method cue above every item.

Top tips

  • Teach each approach first. Interleaving should not disguise missing instruction as desirable difficulty.
  • Remove the topic label. A heading such as “Use this formula” can supply the decision you want students to practise.
  • Ask for classification before execution. Students can state the approach and the clue that makes it suitable before completing the task.
  • Mix related alternatives. Contrast methods students might plausibly confuse, rather than assembling a random set.
  • Expect performance to feel harder. Inspect the nature of errors and provide feedback; difficulty alone is not evidence the sequence is effective.

In the classroom

A Maths practice set mixes tasks requiring different familiar methods. Pupils identify the method and the clue before solving. Feedback compares a plausible alternative with the chosen approach. The difficulty lies partly in selection, whereas a worksheet labelled with one method would have supplied that decision before the pupil began.

Use it for Individual Development

Example goal

I will mix problem types once students can do each separately, so that students practise choosing the right method, not just applying the last one taught.

Peer observer look-fors

  • Practice mixes types that require a real choice.
  • Pupils identify the method before solving.
  • The teacher discusses how to recognise each type.

Quick check of impact

Compare accuracy on a mixed assessment with blocked practice scores.

Adapt the goal to your class and subject. See writing a precise goal and peer observation.
