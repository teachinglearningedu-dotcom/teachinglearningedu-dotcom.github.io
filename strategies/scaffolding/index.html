Scaffolding

Preparing support and deciding when to remove it

Use a scaffold when a specific barrier prevents students from attempting the intended learning successfully. Keep the goal clear and plan how students will work without the support.

How to do it

  1. Diagnose the barrier. Identify whether pupils lack knowledge, cannot coordinate the steps, are overloaded by presentation, or need a cue to select a known process.
  2. Break the process into meaningful steps. Sequence the task so pupils can focus on one decision at a time while still seeing how the parts contribute to the whole.
  3. Select a targeted support. Use a model, worked example, prompt, frame, word bank, visual cue, checklist or partially completed response that addresses the diagnosed barrier.
  4. Keep the thinking with the pupil. The scaffold should cue attention or structure action; it should not complete the crucial reasoning on the pupil's behalf.
  5. Check and adjust. Use responses during practice to decide whether to re-model, retain, reduce or remove the support.
  6. Plan the fade before use. Decide which element will disappear first and what evidence will indicate readiness for the next level of independence.

What it sounds like

“Use this opening frame to state your claim. You will choose the evidence and explain the link yourself; the frame does not supply those decisions.”

Do

  • Match temporary support to the diagnosed need.
  • Keep the curricular goal ambitious.

Avoid

  • Assigning a permanent scaffold by pupil label.
  • Removing every support at once because time has passed.

Top tips

  • Name the barrier. Decide whether the student needs help with knowledge, language, organisation or a particular process step.
  • Scaffold the obstacle. A vocabulary bank helps with expression; it will not repair an untaught concept.
  • Make support optional where appropriate. Students who can already work independently should not have to complete unnecessary scaffolding.
  • Preserve the decision. Give a starting cue or structure without supplying the thinking the task is meant to develop.
  • Plan the withdrawal. Decide what successful performance would justify removing a cue, worked step or sentence frame.

In the classroom

In English, pupils need to explain how evidence supports a claim. A frame supplies the paragraph structure while pupils choose and interpret the quotation. Once they can build the link, the opening phrases are removed. The scaffold supports expression without replacing the interpretation that the task is intended to develop.

Use it for Individual Development

Example goal

I will provide one targeted scaffold for the specific barrier in a task and plan when to remove it, so that students attempt the full learning goal and then succeed without the support.

Peer observer look-fors

  • The scaffold addresses a named barrier (for example, a sentence frame, partial model or step prompt).
  • The task's goal stays the same for all pupils; only the support changes.
  • Some pupils work without the scaffold, or with less of it, later in the lesson or sequence.

Quick check of impact

Compare the quality of work produced with the scaffold and later without it. Note how many pupils no longer need it.

Adapt the goal to your class and subject. See writing a precise goal and peer observation.
