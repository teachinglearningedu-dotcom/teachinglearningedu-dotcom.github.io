Cognitive simplicity

Designing slides and resources that support attention

Use cognitive simplicity when slides or resources compete with the explanation. Reduce unnecessary searching, reading and distraction so students can attend to the content that matters.

How to do it

  1. Identify the essential information. Decide what students need to see, hear or read at the current point.
  2. Remove competing material. Delete decorative elements, irrelevant detail and duplicated language that adds no explanatory value.
  3. Reduce searching. Group related content, label clearly and keep the important feature easy to locate.
  4. Sequence the reveal. Introduce information in coherent chunks and separate reading, listening and copying when they would compete.
  5. Test the resource. Check readability from the back of the room and whether students can locate the reference needed during practice.

What it sounds like

“Read these two sentences silently first. I will then explain the distinction. Watch the highlighted phrase as we connect it to the diagram.”

Do

  • Use layout and sequence to direct attention.
  • Keep text and visuals purposeful.

Avoid

  • Decorative images, clutter and redundant text.
  • Removing essential information merely to make a slide look sparse.

Top tips

  • Remove decorative competition. An irrelevant picture or animated effect can attract attention away from the learning feature.
  • Place related information together. Keep a label beside its diagram feature and a condition close to the step it affects.
  • Reveal deliberately. A completed diagram or crowded page may demand more processing than the current explanation requires.
  • Stop talking over new reading. Give students time to inspect substantial unfamiliar text before explaining it.
  • Preserve useful reference material. Simplicity does not mean removing the model or vocabulary students need to practise successfully.

In the classroom

A teacher removes an unrelated image, moves labels beside the diagram and reveals one part at a time. Students read a short definition in silence before the teacher explains its connection to the visual. The revised resource reduces competing demands while retaining the reference information pupils need during practice.

Use it for Individual Development

Example goal

I will simplify slides so that each one shows only what supports my explanation, so that students attend to the key content rather than searching or reading.

Peer observer look-fors

  • Slides have minimal text and no decorative distractions.
  • The teacher talks and the slide supports; pupils are not reading paragraphs.
  • Pupils are looking at the right place at the right time.

Quick check of impact

Compare pupils' recall of a key point from a simplified explanation with a previous version.

Adapt the goal to your class and subject. See writing a precise goal and peer observation.
