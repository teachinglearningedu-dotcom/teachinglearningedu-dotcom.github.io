Examples and non-examples

Clarifying what counts

Use examples and non-examples to make the defining feature of a concept clear. Carefully chosen near-misses help students stop relying on superficial appearance.

How to do it

  1. Identify the critical feature. Name the single feature that determines whether something belongs to the concept.
  2. Choose a near-miss. Use a non-example that shares the example’s surface features but removes the critical feature. Avoid random or obviously wrong cases.
  3. Alternate and vary. Show an example, then a related non-example; change one feature at a time so the contrast is visible.
  4. Include boundary cases. Use cases that are just inside or just outside the concept so students must apply the definition, not guess from appearance.
  5. Pause, commit, reveal. Ask what changed and what stayed the same; have every student decide before revealing and explaining the answer.

What it sounds like

“Both diagrams look similar. Which is a circuit, and which feature decides? Explain the smallest change that would make the other one a valid example.”

Do

  • Use boundary cases and independent decisions.
  • Connect the judgement to the defining feature.

Avoid

  • Obviously irrelevant wrong examples.
  • Changing several features so the critical contrast disappears.

Top tips

  • Choose close contrasts. A near-miss should share enough features with the example to make the boundary worth thinking about.
  • Change one important feature. Too many simultaneous changes make it difficult to see what determines membership.
  • Include unusual valid cases. Prevent students from treating the first familiar example as the definition.
  • Ask for the criterion. “It looks right” is less revealing than a reason linked to the critical feature.
  • Return to the definition. After comparisons, students should be able to describe what counts and test a new case against it.

In the classroom

A Maths teacher contrasts a valid rectangle with a near-miss that shares several visual features. Pupils identify the defining properties rather than rely on familiar orientation. A tilted valid example and a plausible invalid case follow. Students justify each classification, making the concept boundary clearer than a page of identical conventional examples.

Use it for Individual Development

Example goal

I will use carefully chosen examples and near-miss non-examples, so that students can identify what defines the concept rather than rely on surface features.

Peer observer look-fors

  • Examples and non-examples are shown side by side.
  • Non-examples differ in the defining feature.
  • Pupils correctly classify new cases and explain why.

Quick check of impact

Give a short sort task with new cases. Record accuracy and the quality of reasons.

Adapt the goal to your class and subject. See writing a precise goal and peer observation.
