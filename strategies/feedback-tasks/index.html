Feedback tasks

Planning time to act on feedback

Use feedback tasks to make improvement an act of thinking. Select a solvable challenge that helps students locate, explain and repair an issue rather than simply copy a correction.

How to do it

  1. Choose a narrow learning focus. Identify the knowledge, skill or misconception that matters and that pupils can realistically improve.
  2. Record what needs fixing without annotating it. Mark or review the work, then state the number and type of issues rather than correcting each location.
  3. Pose a solvable search. Tell pupils what they are looking for and what success will look like, but leave the locating and correcting to them.
  4. Add the minimum cue if needed. Narrow the section, paragraph, step or error type without revealing the exact line or answer.
  5. Protect in-class time to act. Return the work when pupils have time to search, think, revise and use relevant models or criteria.
  6. Close the loop. Ask pupils to explain what they changed and why, check the correction and later test whether they can apply the learning independently.

What it sounds like

“Two statements misuse the key term. Find them, explain the issue and correct them using the definition and the model.”

Do

  • Set a bounded, achievable improvement action.
  • Keep pupils doing the cognitive work.

Avoid

  • Cryptic feedback on an error students cannot diagnose.
  • Colour-coded corrections with no new thinking.

Top tips

  • Use detective work when pupils can solve it. Give the number and type of issues, then let students find and fix them.
  • Add the minimum cue. Narrow to a paragraph, step or criterion if the search is too broad.
  • Give direct teaching where needed. An error caused by missing knowledge is not a productive puzzle.
  • Protect action time. Plan redrafting, rehearsal, reattempting or re-testing into the lesson rather than hoping students act later.
  • Require an explanation. Ask what changed and why, then use a related task where the corrected learning matters.

In the classroom

In Languages, the teacher identifies the number and type of errors in a familiar paragraph without correcting each location. Pupils use the model and criteria to find, explain and repair them. When a pupil lacks the relevant rule, the teacher gives direct teaching rather than leave the student in an unproductive search.

Use it for Individual Development

Example goal

I will plan time and tasks for students to act on feedback, so that students think about and improve their work rather than just reading comments.

Peer observer look-fors

  • Lesson time is given to improvement tasks.
  • Tasks require thinking, not copying.
  • Pupils make meaningful changes.

Quick check of impact

Look at the proportion of pupils whose work improves after feedback tasks.

Adapt the goal to your class and subject. See writing a precise goal and peer observation.
