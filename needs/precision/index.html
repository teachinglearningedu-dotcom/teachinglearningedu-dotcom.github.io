Pupil need

Pupils' answers are short or imprecise

Answers are one word, vague or partly correct, and pupils rarely use subject vocabulary accurately.

Where to start

Raise the expectation of what a complete answer sounds like, give pupils the words to say it, and ask them to improve their own answer.

Strategies to consider

Choose one. Start with a core strategy unless another fits your class better.

  1. Probing questions Research-informed

    Use probing questions after students have relevant knowledge to deepen or clarify the reasoning behind an answer. Plan a focused follow-up rather than asking a vague “Why?” every time.

    Example goal: I will ask a planned follow-up question after initial answers, so that students explain their reasoning and develop more precise understanding.

  2. Precise answers / Don’t round up Established practice

    Use precise-answer routines when a partly correct response is being accepted as complete. Help the student improve the answer rather than silently replacing it with your own explanation.

    Example goal: I will ask students to improve partly correct answers rather than finishing them myself, so that students learn to give precise, complete responses.

  3. Explicit vocabulary teaching Research-informedCore strategy

    Use explicit vocabulary teaching when disciplinary language is essential to understanding and expressing an idea. Students need to hear, say, use and revisit the word in context.

    Example goal: I will explicitly teach two or three keystone words per topic, with student-friendly definitions and repeated use, so that students understand and use subject vocabulary accurately in speech and writing.

  4. Oracy for learning Research-informed

    Use oracy routines to teach speaking and listening as skills that support disciplinary thinking. Clarify whether students are exploring an idea or presenting a developed explanation.

    Example goal: I will explicitly teach and model one oracy skill, such as building on others' ideas, so that students' discussion becomes more structured and subject-specific.

  5. Compare justify evaluate Research-informed

    Use comparison, justification and evaluation prompts when students need to explain a relationship or make a judgement. Ensure they have the knowledge and criteria required to reason well.

    Example goal: I will use compare, justify and evaluate questions once students have the necessary knowledge, so that students reason more deeply and support their judgements.

  6. Checks for listening Established practice

    Use brief checks to establish whether students heard the instruction, key explanation point or contribution from a classmate. This helps distinguish missed information from difficulty understanding it.

    Example goal: I will check that students have heard key instructions and classmates' contributions by asking a quick follow-up, so that students listen actively and act on what was said.
