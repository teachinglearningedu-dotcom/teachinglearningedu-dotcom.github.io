Pupil need

Pupils don't follow my explanations

Explanations feel clear to you, but pupils lose the thread, cannot repeat the key idea or need it explained again.

Where to start

Reduce what pupils have to hold in mind at once. Break content into small steps, make expert thinking visible and remove anything that competes for attention.

Strategies to consider

Choose one. Start with a core strategy unless another fits your class better.

  1. Small steps Strong evidence baseCore strategy

    Use small steps when several interacting decisions make new content hard to follow. Teach a coherent component, rehearse it and then connect it to the next part.

    Example goal: I will teach new content in small steps, checking each one before adding the next, so that students follow the explanation and can carry out each step accurately.

  2. Explaining with precision Research-informed

    Use precise explanation when the content is correct but the message is too long, unclear or difficult to follow. Plan what to say, what to show and what to leave out.

    Example goal: I will script and rehearse the core of key explanations, cutting unnecessary detail, so that students follow the explanation and can repeat the key idea accurately.

  3. Think aloud Strong evidence baseCore strategy

    Use a think aloud when the important learning sits in the choices an expert makes. Show how you plan, monitor and evaluate, including why you reject an attractive alternative.

    Example goal: I will think aloud while modelling, making my planning, monitoring and checking explicit, so that students understand the expert decisions and use them in their own work.

  4. The visualiser Research-informed

    Use the visualiser to show how a response is constructed, including decisions, annotations and revisions. Students see the sequence that a finished example can hide.

    Example goal: I will model answers live under the visualiser, narrating each decision, so that students see how a strong response is built and can construct their own.

  5. Cognitive simplicity Research-informed

    Use cognitive simplicity when slides or resources compete with the explanation. Reduce unnecessary searching, reading and distraction so students can attend to the content that matters.

    Example goal: I will simplify slides so that each one shows only what supports my explanation, so that students attend to the key content rather than searching or reading.

  6. Dual coding Research-informed

    Use words and visuals together when a diagram makes a relationship, sequence or structure easier to understand. Select the representation for its explanatory purpose.

    Example goal: I will pair key explanations with a simple diagram that shows the structure or sequence, so that students understand and remember the relationships more clearly.

  7. Examples and non-examples Research-informed

    Use examples and non-examples to make the defining feature of a concept clear. Carefully chosen near-misses help students stop relying on superficial appearance.

    Example goal: I will use carefully chosen examples and near-miss non-examples, so that students can identify what defines the concept rather than rely on surface features.
