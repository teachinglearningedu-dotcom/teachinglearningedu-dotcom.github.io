Pupil need

Pupils forget what they have been taught

Pupils can answer soon after teaching but struggle weeks later, or start new topics without the knowledge they need.

Where to start

Forgetting is normal. Retrieval and spacing make knowledge more durable and show you what needs revisiting. Start by making the Do Now genuine retrieval from memory, then plan when key knowledge comes back.

Strategies to consider

Choose one. Start with a core strategy unless another fits your class better.

  1. Do Now Research-informedCore strategy

    At BSCS, Do Now provides a short, independent start using spaced retrieval of previously taught, curriculum-critical knowledge. Keep the routine stable so thinking begins without a fresh explanation.

    Example goal: I will make the Do Now spaced retrieval of curriculum-critical knowledge that students complete independently in silence, so that students start thinking immediately and strengthen long-term recall.

  2. Retrieval practice Strong evidence baseCore strategy

    Use retrieval practice to strengthen access to previously taught knowledge. Students should attempt to generate the answer from memory before they see notes, prompts or the solution.

    Example goal: I will have students retrieve key knowledge from memory before showing answers or notes, so that students strengthen recall and I can see what has been retained.

  3. Spaced retrieval Strong evidence baseCore strategy

    Use spaced retrieval when students can answer soon after teaching but struggle weeks later. Plan repeated returns to important knowledge rather than treating a topic as finished.

    Example goal: I will plan returns to key knowledge at increasing intervals, so that students can recall it weeks later, not just soon after teaching.

  4. Low-stakes quizzes Strong evidence base

    Use a recurring low-stakes quiz to revisit knowledge across topics and identify what each student needs to retrieve again. The review matters as much as the score.

    Example goal: I will use a regular low-stakes quiz across topics with a quick review, so that students practise recall and know what they need to revisit.

  5. Review Cards Research-informed

    Use Review Cards to turn corrected errors into a personal retrieval resource. Separate the question from the solution so students can attempt it again without seeing the answer.

    Example goal: I will have students turn corrected errors into review cards for later retrieval, so that students revisit and secure the knowledge they got wrong.

  6. Getting students on board with retrieval Research-informed

    Use an explanation and demonstration of retrieval when students see quizzes as judgement or prefer familiar rereading. Help them understand why attempting recall is part of learning.

    Example goal: I will explain and demonstrate why retrieval works, so that students attempt recall before checking notes and use retrieval when revising independently.
