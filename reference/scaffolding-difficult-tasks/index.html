Scaffolding difficult tasks

Temporary support such as a model, worked example, prompt, sentence stem, vocabulary bank, partial task or visual cue that preserves the ambitious task.

What it is: Temporary support such as a model, worked example, prompt, sentence stem, vocabulary bank, partial task or visual cue that preserves the ambitious task.

Why it helps: A scaffold reduces an avoidable barrier or part of the load while keeping attention on the intended thinking. Its success is measured by the independence it enables.

Use it when: when the goal is appropriate but students cannot yet access a step because of prior knowledge, language, executive or processing demands.

Implementation sequence

Identify the exact barrier and the knowledge students already possess.

Choose the least support that gives a viable route into the task.

Explain how to use the scaffold and model it briefly.

Monitor whether it improves accuracy and thinking.

Reduce or remove support as soon as evidence permits.

DODON'T
Plan the fade at the same time as the scaffold. Use different levels of support flexibly within the same goal. Ask students which support helped and why. Simplify the learning outcome or remove the hard thinking. Give every student the same permanent scaffold. Mistake task completion with a scaffold for independent mastery.

Check for impact

Students complete the same intellectual goal with less support over time.

The scaffold addresses the intended barrier rather than adding extra steps.

Sources informing this profile: DistilED: Provide Scaffolds for Difficult Tasks; BSCS Adaptive Teaching.
