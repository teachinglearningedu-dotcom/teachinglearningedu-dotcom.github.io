Core idea

Cold Call means asking a question and then selecting a pupil regardless of whether their hand is raised. Used routinely, it raises the participation, thinking and listening ratio, gives the teacher a more representative sample of understanding, and makes mistakes feel less exceptional.

Routine

  • 1. Ask the question to the whole class first.
  • 2. Pause for thinking time.
  • 3. Then name the pupil: question → pause → name.
  • 4. Expect a complete response where the task demands it, including accurate disciplinary vocabulary.
  • 5. Use the answer to decide the next question or whether to broaden the sample.

Best uses

  • After Silent Solo or Everybody Writes.
  • After Turn and Talk, so the selected pupil has rehearsed an answer.
  • During explanation to check listening.
  • To sample understanding from different parts of the room rather than relying on the same volunteers.
  • To ask pupils to build on, compare or evaluate another answer.

Do / avoid

DoAvoid
Ask before naming.Naming first, which lets everyone else stop thinking.
Use enough wait time that everyone has a real chance to think.Using Cold Call mainly as a behaviour trap.
Use it frequently enough that it becomes normal.Calling only on pupils likely to be correct.
Pair it with rehearsal when the response is demanding.Assuming one cold-called answer represents the whole class.
Sample strategically across the class.Removing wait time in the name of pace.

Explicit link to the BSCS model

  • Drive thinking for all: Cold Call makes participation less dependent on volunteering and keeps everyone accountable.
  • Check and adapt: It can provide a quick sample, although whole-class methods such as MWBs are stronger when the decision requires reliable class-wide data.
  • Explain: Useful for checks for listening and brief recall without converting I Do into a long dialogue.
  • Turn and Talk: A strong sequence is Think / Write → Turn and Talk → Cold Call.
