Core idea

Retain the valuable feature of a walking-talking mock - hearing an expert explain their thinking - but replace passive copying with a responsive cycle: pupils attempt, commit and rate confidence; the teacher diagnoses; pupils receive only the explanation they need; understanding is checked again; secure pupils are stretched; and errors become future retrieval material.

Why the conventional version can fail

  • Pupils may copy without being cognitively active.
  • The teacher gathers no usable data, so every question receives similar time regardless of need.
  • Silence, nodding and neat notes create an illusion of understanding for teachers and pupils.
  • Pupils who are already secure gain little from repeated full explanations.
  • An annotated paper is difficult to use for practice testing and can default to rereading.

A responsive walking-talking mock sequence

  • 1. Attempt first. Work through one question at a time. Give slightly less than normal exam time so pace stays purposeful and every pupil attempts the question before seeing a model.
  • 2. Commit confidence. Before any answer is revealed, pupils record a 0-10 confidence score next to their response. This increases attention to feedback and identifies confidently held errors.
  • 3. Gather whole-class evidence. Pupils show the final answer on mini-whiteboards. For multi-step questions, ask for the first line of working using Step-by-Step. If boards are blank, ask pupils to identify the topic or first decision. In a hall, allocate staff a zone to scan.
  • 4. Respond proportionately. At roughly 80% or more correct, confirm or model briefly and preserve time for later questions. When understanding is less secure, boards down and hands empty: model the solution and narrate the thinking at each step.
  • 5. Re-check after explanation. Use a fresh follow-up question with the same context and comparable difficulty but enough variation to require thinking. Shadow-paper questions are useful. Re-check on mini-whiteboards. If pupils are still insecure, record the topic for fuller reteaching rather than pretending the mock alone has fixed it.
  • 6. Stretch secure pupils. Use a What If question that changes a condition, representation, required form or mark-scheme decision while preserving the underlying concept.
  • 7. Create Review Cards. Begin with each pupil's highest-confidence error. On the front, copy the question. On the back, record the topic, where to find further help or practice, the original mistake, a corrected worked example and annotations for the pupil's 'forgetful future self'. A practical target is three cards in ten minutes.
  • 8. Re-quiz later. Pupils answer the front of a card before checking the back. Correct cards move to the bottom of the deck; incorrect cards are studied and returned nearer the top so they recur sooner.

Explicit link to the BSCS model

  • Check and adapt: Mini-whiteboards and confidence scores reveal the class pattern; explanation time and the next question change in response.
  • Feedback to improve: The model, equivalent re-check and Review Card make feedback something pupils act on rather than merely receive.
  • Secure learning through practice: Pupils attempt before modelling, complete a fresh follow-up and then revisit errors through retrieval.
  • Drive thinking for all: Every pupil commits to an answer and confidence rating; staff scan all responses rather than relying on volunteers.
  • Lesson preparation: Select the questions, timing, diagnostic response, shadow/follow-up questions, What If variations and Review Card support references in advance.

Boundary: Barton's examples are rooted in mathematics. Preserve the active-attempt, diagnosis, re-check and revision principles when adapting to other subjects, but redesign the response format for the demands of that subject's exam questions.
